我的第一次实习是在一家位于美国怀俄明州的 AI 创业公司。

名义上，我是 AI Research 实习生；实际上，我做的更接近 AI 产品工作。

有一天，我申请从大办公室搬到小办公室。原因很实际：大办公室的人通常下午才到，而我每天早上已经开始工作。他们下班也晚，我总觉得自己先走不太合适。

小办公室的人来得更早。我想，搬过去以后，也许就能在比较正常的时间结束一天。

这只是一次很小的座位调整，却让我看见了一种之后一直在思考的组织方式。

## 两个办公室，两条时间线

搬过去之后我发现，它们不只是两个不同的房间，更像是活在公司的不同版本里。

假设当前产品正在 v1.0。大办公室的人在运行 v1.0、开发 v2.0；小办公室的人已经在讨论 v4.0，甚至开始设想 v5.0。

大办公室由经验丰富的全职团队构成，负责把已经成立的产品继续建设、发布和支持。小办公室则是几位 AI PM 实习生，我们的任务是探索。

它们不是重复的两套团队，而是同一家公司里的两个飞轮：

- **当下的飞轮**：建设、发布、支持，让已经成立的产品稳定向前。
- **未来的飞轮**：观察、系统化、探索，为还没有准备好进入产品路线图的问题保留空间。

## 一种刻意保留的不对齐

大多数组织把对齐视为理所当然的好事。但这个结构刻意保留了一点不对齐。

如果探索和交付被放进同一套日常节奏里，探索很容易被交接、排期和线上问题吞掉。紧急的事情几乎总会先被处理，而重要但暂时不紧急的事情，很少拥有真正属于自己的时间。

小办公室的价值不在于制造更多“未来概念”，而在于保护一种不同的工作节奏。

这里的工作不需要立刻交付，也不需要提前证明一定会被采用。但它必须持续接近新的技术和真实问题。

这种被保护起来的距离，让团队可以看得更远，同时不至于完全脱离当下。

## 没有人分配过的三个角色

我搬进小办公室之前，那里有两位实习生；搬进去以后，我们变成三个人。

名义上，我们的任务是给创始人提供新的想法。这很合理，因为创始人本身也是公司里最核心的产品判断者。我们的作用不是代替他的判断，而是扩大公司能够看到的范围。

因为每个人的时间安排比较灵活，关注点也不同，三个角色逐渐自然形成：

1. **Scout**：持续追踪前沿 AI 进展，把原始信号翻译成团队能够理解和使用的输入。
2. **Systematizer**：把零散想法整理成可复用的 SOP、方法和工作流。
3. **Bridge**：把这些探索带进产品与交付团队，再把用户和社区的真实反馈带回探索中。

它们共同形成了一条链路：

`前沿 → 系统 → 一线`

但这不是单向传递。

Scout 带回新的可能性，Systematizer 让它们变得可用，Bridge 再把它们放进真实工作中接受检验。从用户和产品团队返回的信息，又会改变 Scout 下一步关注什么。

这种往返让未来飞轮不会变成脱离现实的想象，也让当下飞轮不会永远被今天的优先级困住。

## 一个办公室守住当下，另一个跑向未来

我并不认为每家公司都需要真的设置两个办公室。

但 AI 创业公司，以及正在经历 AI 转型的组织，都需要保护一块面向未来的空间，让重要但暂时不紧急的工作能够继续存在。

这不一定需要额外的人头，也不一定需要复杂的组织设计。

更重要的是保留一种不同的时间尺度，并让追踪前沿的人、负责系统化的人，以及最接近产品和用户的人之间保持持续往返。

一个飞轮守住当下，另一个飞轮跑向未来。

两者都不能停。

My first internship was at an AI startup in Wyoming.

On paper, I was an AI Research intern. In practice, I was doing product work.

One day, I asked to move from the larger office to the smaller one. The reason was practical. The people in the larger office usually arrived in the afternoon, while I had already been working since morning. They also stayed late, and I kept feeling awkward when I left before them.

The people in the smaller office came in earlier. I thought that moving there might let me leave at a more normal hour.

It was a small decision about where to sit. But the move showed me a way of organizing a company that I have kept thinking about ever since.

## Two offices, two timelines

After I moved, I realized that the offices were not merely separated by space. They seemed to inhabit different versions of the company.

Suppose the current product was at v1.0.

The larger office was operating v1.0 and building v2.0. The smaller office was already discussing v4.0 and imagining v5.0.

The larger office was made up of experienced full-time teams. They built, shipped, and supported a product that had already earned its place.

The smaller office was a few AI PM interns. Our job was exploration.

These were not two versions of the same team. They were two flywheels inside one company:

- **The present flywheel:** build, ship, and support what already works.
- **The future flywheel:** observe, systematize, and explore questions that are not yet ready for the main product roadmap.

## A deliberate misalignment

Most organizations treat alignment as an unquestioned good. This structure preserved a small amount of misalignment on purpose.

When exploration and delivery compete inside the same day-to-day rhythm, exploration is easily consumed by handoffs, schedules, and production issues.

Urgent work nearly always gets handled first. Important work without an immediate deadline rarely receives time that truly belongs to it.

The smaller office was valuable not because it produced more futuristic concepts, but because it protected a different working rhythm.

The work did not have to ship immediately. It did not have to prove upfront that it would be adopted. It did, however, have to stay close to new technology and real problems.

That protected distance allowed the team to look further ahead without losing contact with the present.

## The roles nobody assigned

Before I moved into the smaller office, there were two interns there. Afterward, there were three of us.

Officially, we were there to bring the founder new ideas. That made sense: he was also the company's central product thinker. Our role was not to replace his judgment, but to widen what the company could see.

Because our schedules were flexible and our interests differed, three roles gradually emerged without anyone designing them in advance.

1. **The Scout** followed frontier AI developments and translated raw signals into something the team could understand.
2. **The Systematizer** turned scattered ideas into reusable SOPs, methods, and workflows.
3. **The Bridge** carried those ideas into product and delivery teams, then brought user and community feedback back into exploration.

Together, they formed a natural chain:

`Frontier → systems → frontline`

But the movement was not one-way.

The Scout brought in new possibilities. The Systematizer made them usable. The Bridge tested them against operating reality. What came back from users and product teams changed what the Scout looked for next.

That exchange kept the future flywheel from becoming detached speculation, and kept the present flywheel from becoming trapped inside today's priorities.

## One office holds the present; the other runs toward the future

I do not think every company needs two literal offices.

But AI startups—and organizations moving through an AI transformation—need to protect some space for work that is important without yet being urgent.

This does not necessarily require extra headcount or an elaborate organizational design.

What matters is preserving a different time horizon, then maintaining continuous exchange between the people who scout, the people who systematize, and the people closest to product and users.

One flywheel holds the present. The other runs toward the future.

Neither can stop.
