2023 到 2030 年可能是 AI 最狂野的一段时间。模型、产品形态和公司会快速更替，但这一轮变化留给我们这一代人的，可能不是某个具体工具，而是两种更长久的习惯：使用个人邮箱，以及为软件持续付费。

这个判断背后有一个更基本的观察：**技术变化得很快，也会越来越无感；人的习惯形成得慢，却可能持续十年甚至二十年。**

## 技术进步，往往意味着操作消失

早期的 Vibe Coding 是一件有明显技术门槛的事。除了 PRD，人通常还需要准备技术设计和任务清单，明确依赖、涉及文件和验收标准。你不能完全依赖 AI 写这些内容，也不能完全看不懂它们。

那是一个“有感”的阶段：人通过大量显式输入，维持 Agent 执行的连贯性、可验收性和可证伪性。

后来，模型能力和 Agent harness 一起变化。完整的前期规划仍然可能在局部执行中失效，Agent 却开始更主动地建立技术方案、验收标准和测试。PRD、技术设计和任务清单没有突然失去价值；其中一部分只是从用户必须亲手完成的工作，变成了 Agent 的内部过程。

人感知到的操作减少了，技术在背后承担的工作反而更多。

我现在“写”文章也是类似的过程。这里的“写”需要打引号：很多初稿来自 Typeless 口述。我更多是在表达和判断，技术负责把声音变成可以继续编辑的文字。写作并没有消失，但人与写作工具之间的分工改变了。

## 技术可以消失，习惯不会立刻消失

界面变简单，不代表人会立即放弃过去形成的工作方式。

即使 Agent 能每天自动整理邮件，人也可能继续在早上打开邮箱；即使一个新工具能把任务主动送到面前，人仍会沿用自己熟悉的收件箱、文件夹和通知路径。

这不是用户不够先进。习惯本身就是一种基础设施：它降低每天重新决定“我该怎么做”的成本。产品可以在几个月里升级，人的默认动作却需要很长时间才会移动。

## 第一个变化：个人邮箱可能重新进入国内日常

长期使用 Claude Code、Codex 或全球化 AI 产品的人，很容易重新频繁接触邮箱。验证码、产品通知、账单、邀请和开发者服务经常优先进入 Gmail 等邮箱体系，而不是手机号或某个国内平台账号。

一开始，邮箱可能只是注册工具。使用次数增加以后，它会重新成为一种跨平台身份：一个人可以携带它进入不同服务，不必把身份完全寄托在单一社交平台上。

这不意味着邮箱会取代微信，也不意味着国内一定会出现新的邮箱巨头。更值得观察的是：当 Agent 需要代表一个人访问多个服务时，邮箱是否会重新成为定义“这个人是谁”的一部分。如果答案是肯定的，围绕邮箱的身份、通知、支付和 Agent 服务就可能重新生长。

## 第二个变化：为软件付费开始成为默认选项

我第一次为 AI 产品付费，是为了开始使用 Claude Code。后来我也为 Manus 做过一次昂贵得多的尝试，现在则已经习惯为 Codex 持续支付订阅费用。

其中有些钱花得值，有些显然没有。但更重要的变化是：我不再默认软件应该先免费，再通过广告或流量变现。只要产品持续提供清楚的价值，订阅和按调用量付费都开始变得自然。

这种习惯一旦形成，并不会只留在 AI 产品里。用户可能开始接受为其他小型软件按月支付十几元或几十元，也更容易理解“没有广告，但需要付费”是一种正常交换。

AI 产品训练的，可能不只是使用 Agent 的能力，也包括一代人对软件价值的基本预期。

## 我们是过渡一代，不是完全的 AI 原生代

这里的“我们”，主要指 00 后，也包括现在处在青少年阶段的一部分 10 后。我们在大学、研究生或刚进入工作时遇到 AI，因此它会深刻改变我们的工作与消费习惯。

但严格来说，我们并不是完全的 AI 原生代。我们经历过电视、电脑、智能手机，再进入 Agent 时代。AI 是后来进入生活的技术，而不是从出生起就存在的环境。

真正从一开始就把 AI 视为空气的人，可能是 20 后、30 后，以及更晚出生的一代。他们未必继承我们今天的邮箱和订阅习惯，也可能发明完全不同的默认方式。

这使我们处在一个有趣的位置：十五到二十年后，我们可能成为 AI 原生时代的建设者，却仍带着在互联网和早期 Agent 时代形成的动作与判断。

## 观察习惯，而不只是追踪功能

判断 AI 的长期影响，不能只看哪个模型更强、哪个产品又增加了什么按钮。功能可以在一次发布中出现，也可以在下一次发布中消失。

更耐久的信号是：人开始默认用什么身份进入服务，愿意为什么付钱，把哪些判断交给 Agent，又在哪些地方坚持自己完成。

技术最终会退到幕后。但由技术塑造的习惯，可能比技术本身存在得更久。

The years from 2023 to 2030 may become the wildest phase of AI. Models, product forms, and companies will change quickly. What this period leaves behind, however, may not be one particular tool. It may be two durable habits: using personal email and paying continuously for software.

That prediction rests on a more basic observation: **technology changes quickly and becomes less visible; habits form slowly and may last for decades.**

## Progress often looks like operations disappearing

Early vibe coding had a visible technical threshold. A PRD was rarely enough. People prepared technical designs and task lists that specified dependencies, files, and acceptance criteria. You could not rely entirely on AI to produce those materials, and you could not safely ignore what they contained.

This was a high-friction phase. Humans supplied a large amount of explicit structure to keep execution coherent, testable, and falsifiable.

As models and agent harnesses improved, the division of work began to move. Detailed upfront planning could still fail during local execution, while agents became more capable of proposing technical approaches, forming acceptance criteria, and running tests. PRDs and technical plans did not lose their value. Some of the work simply moved from an artifact the user had to prepare into a process the agent performed internally.

The user saw fewer operations while the technology did more behind the scenes.

I now “write” in a similar way. The quotation marks matter: many first drafts begin as speech through Typeless. I spend more of my attention on expression and judgment, while the technology turns voice into material I can edit. Writing has not disappeared. The boundary between writer and tool has changed.

## Technology can disappear before habits do

A simpler interface does not make people abandon a familiar working rhythm overnight.

An agent may summarize every message automatically, yet someone may still begin the morning by opening an inbox. A product may proactively deliver the next task, yet the user may continue to organize life through folders, notifications, and routines learned years earlier.

This is not a failure to become sufficiently advanced. Habit is infrastructure too. It removes the cost of deciding, every day, how to do ordinary things. A product can change within months; a person's default motions move much more slowly.

## First possibility: personal email returns to everyday life in China

People who use Claude Code, Codex, and globally distributed AI products often encounter email again and again. Verification codes, product notifications, bills, invitations, and developer services frequently arrive through Gmail and other email systems rather than a phone number or one domestic platform account.

At first, email may be only a registration tool. With repetition, it can become a portable identity: something a person carries across services without placing the whole self inside one social platform.

This does not mean email will replace WeChat, or that a new Chinese email giant is inevitable. The more useful question is whether email becomes part of the answer to “who is this person?” when an agent needs to work across services on their behalf. If it does, identity, notification, payment, and agent services may begin growing around it again.

## Second possibility: paying for software becomes a default option

My first AI subscription was the price of entry to Claude Code. Later I made a much more expensive experiment with Manus. I now treat a continuing Codex subscription as an ordinary software expense.

Some of that money was well spent; some clearly was not. The deeper change is that I no longer assume software must be free first and funded by advertising or traffic later. When a product delivers continuing, legible value, subscriptions and usage-based pricing begin to feel normal.

That habit will not remain confined to AI. A user who learns to pay for an agent may become more willing to spend a small amount each month on other useful software, and more comfortable with a product that has no ads but does have a price.

AI products may be training a generation not only in how to use agents, but in what software is worth.

## We are a transition generation, not a fully AI-native one

By “we,” I mostly mean people born in the 2000s, along with some teenagers born in the 2010s. We encountered AI during university, graduate school, or the beginning of work. It will strongly shape our professional and purchasing habits.

But we are not fully AI native. We moved through television, personal computers, smartphones, and then agents. AI entered our lives later; it was not part of the environment from birth.

Children born in the 2020s, 2030s, and beyond may treat AI as ordinary background infrastructure. They may not inherit today's email and subscription habits at all. They may create a different set of defaults.

That puts our generation in a curious position. Fifteen or twenty years from now, we may be building the AI-native era while still carrying motions and judgments learned during the internet and early-agent eras.

## Watch habits, not only features

The long-term effect of AI cannot be understood only by tracking which model is strongest or which feature appeared this week. Features can arrive in one release and disappear in the next.

The more durable signals are what identity people use to enter a service, what they become willing to pay for, which judgments they hand to an agent, and where they continue to insist on acting themselves.

Technology eventually moves into the background. The habits it creates may remain long after the technology that formed them has changed.
